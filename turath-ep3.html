The Fortress That Fell: The Battle for Santa Cruz

Episode 3 · The Saadians | Documentary series

Santa Cruz: The Fortress That Fell to the People of Sous

Nov 5, 2026 · Zakaria Habbad

More Than a Viewpoint

Every visitor to Agadir climbs to the Kasbah of Agadir Oufella for the view. Most see crumbling walls and the scars of the 1960 earthquake. Few know that this hill was built for war.

Imagine a Portuguese fortress below, armed with heavy cannon and the latest weapons of its time. Around it, thousands of ordinary men from Sous, carrying swords and spears. They were outgunned in every way, and they came back anyway, year after year.

In the third episode of Turath Ummati, we go back to the story of Santa Cruz: a fortress, a siege, and a victory that opened a new chapter in Moroccan history.

A Coast Left Open

In the early 1500s, Morocco was weak from within. The Wattasid dynasty had lost the tribes' trust after its defeats against Spain, and its rule had become little more than symbolic. Each tribe governed itself by its own customs.

Portugal took advantage. With a powerful fleet and skilled navigators, it built fortresses along the Atlantic coast: Anfa, Mazagan, Safi, and Santa Cruz. Spain and the Ottomans were also eyeing Moroccan land.

1505: A Fortress Around a Spring

In 1505 a Portuguese adventurer landed on the coast between the Tamraght and Souss rivers. He found a calm bay, a plain made for a port, and a spring of fresh water: a fonte de água. Five centuries later, one of Agadir's best-known neighbourhoods still carries the name: Founti.

Around that spring he built a heavily armed fortress. When the first curious locals came close, they were met with cannon fire. The newcomers had not come to talk. In 1511 the King of Portugal bought the fortress outright and turned it into a fortified town: Santa Cruz do Cabo de Gué.

The episode also visits Imouran, near Tamraght, where the ruins of a second Portuguese fort are slowly disappearing, unmarked and largely forgotten.

Decades of Unequal War

The tribes of Sous answered by uniting. Near Taroudant they pledged allegiance to a sharif, al-Qa'im bi-Amr Allah, as leader of the struggle against the Portuguese. It was the beginning of the Saadian dynasty, and of a long war.

It was a war of swords against cannon. One Portuguese soldier could kill ten or twenty attackers before they got close. Villages across the region were raided again and again, from Tamraght to Massa.

The episode tells the stories that turned the tide: a local leader who chose the Portuguese side and paid for it, a young refugee couple welcomed into the fortress on a moonlit night, and a revenge raid from the Canary Islands so brutal that it sealed the fortress's fate.

A Truce That Was Not a Surrender

After years of losses, the Saadian leader Mohammed al-Sheikh did something no one expected: he offered the Portuguese a six-year truce. A similar truce had cost the Wattasids the trust of the tribes forever. Was he about to make the same mistake?

He was not. During those six years he built a stronghold on the hill overlooking Santa Cruz. That stronghold is the kasbah visitors climb today.

March 1541

When the truce ended, Santa Cruz woke up surrounded. Tents, horsemen and cannon pulled by camels covered the horizon, a force the chroniclers put at more than 100,000 fighters. Inside the walls, the Portuguese sent letter after letter to their king. None were answered.

What happened next involves gunpowder that would not ignite, soldiers who believed they were fighting jinn, and a commander who handed over his own turban as a promise. The full story, with the places where it happened, is in the episode.
